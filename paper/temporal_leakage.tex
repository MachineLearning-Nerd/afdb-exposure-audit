\begin{abstract}
Comparisons of AlphaFold Database models with experimental structures underpin
knowledge of pLDDT, AlphaFold's predicted local distance difference test
(lDDT). Two problems can distort them silently: exposure, when the reference
or a close relative was available to the model in training or as a template,
and wrong residue correspondence, when residues are paired with wrong
reference atoms. Of 868 ATLAS proteins, 91\% had no reference released after
AlphaFold2's training cutoff. In the first four batches of a preregistered
census of X-ray PDB entries released after 2026-09-11, 87 of 138 proteins
(63\%) had a same-protein or $\ge$95\%-identical structure predating the
cutoff, and 45 models (33\%) were given a same-protein template. In an
exploratory census, proteins with such relatives had slightly higher lDDT and
pLDDT and no detectable difference in confident errors (pLDDT $\ge$ 90, lDDT
$<$ 0.60). In a published date-split test set, at least 30 of 82 chains had
such a relative; in a published template-free comparison, accuracy was not
clearly higher with one. For some chains our pipeline mapped residues in label
numbering but read atoms by author numbering. Correction changed the median
lDDT of 188 of 488 chain segments and removed spurious confident errors
mimicking overconfidence. A preregistered test of conformal intervals for
lDDT, which does not use pLDDT, failed on original values; we withdraw that
outcome. A rerun registered after the error and a preliminary result were
known meets the rule on corrected values: mean coverage 0.8945 and 0.9456 at
nominal 0.90 and 0.95 (thresholds 0.8914 and 0.9449).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

AlphaFold2 \citep{jumper2021} and the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database
(AFDB; \citealp{varadi2022,bertoni2026}) have made predicted structures
available for hundreds of millions of proteins. For each residue, AlphaFold
reports a confidence score that predicts the local distance difference test
(lDDT), a superposition-free score of local agreement between a model and a
reference structure \citep{mariani2013}. The score is called predicted lDDT
(pLDDT), and much of what is known about it comes from comparing AFDB models
with other data on the same protein. Comparisons with experimental
structures, simulations and disorder annotations ask how well pLDDT tracks
local error \citep{jumper2021}, whether it reflects flexibility or disorder
\citep{akdel2022,ruff2021,guo2022,saldano2022,carugo2023}, and whether
conformal intervals built on pLDDT reach their nominal coverage
\citep{shihab2026calpro}. Two problems can make such comparisons misleading
without stopping the pipeline. Agreement can look too good because the model
has seen the reference or something close to it. It can look too bad because
model residues were paired with the wrong reference atoms.

The first problem is \emph{exposure}: the reference or a close relative
could have informed the model, as a training example, as a
\emph{template} (a known structure given to the network as input at
prediction time), or as a close homologue in the training data. For some
questions, such as describing AFDB as it is used, exposure need not invalidate the comparison.
For others, such as accuracy on new proteins or the calibration of pLDDT on
unseen folds, agreement with an exposed reference may reflect recall rather
than generalisation. When the claim is generalisation beyond exposed targets, this is a form of data leakage \citep{kaufman2012,kapoor2023},
and we call the routes by which it can enter an AFDB comparison
\emph{exposure channels}. Structure prediction has long guarded against
leakage through blind and time-split evaluation
\citep{kryshtafovych2021,haas2018,jumper2021}, and recent benchmarks show
that release-date splits alone still leak through homology
\citep{buttenschoen2024,durairaj2024,dobson2025beta,bushuiev2024}. In a
retrospective AFDB comparison the model is fixed and the templates it was given
are recorded in its model file, so template exposure can be measured rather than assumed.

The second problem is \emph{residue correspondence}. Model residues are
numbered by their position in the UniProt sequence. Reference residues are
numbered within an entry of the Protein Data Bank (PDB), whose mmCIF
(macromolecular Crystallographic Information File) format carries two
schemes: a sequential \emph{label numbering} (\texttt{label\_seq\_id}) and an
\emph{author numbering} (\texttt{auth\_seq\_id}) chosen by the depositors
\citep{westbrook2022,rcsbidentifiers,mmcifdict}. Resources such as SIFTS
(Structure Integration with Function, Taxonomy and Sequences), distributed by
the Protein Data Bank in Europe (PDBe), map these numberings to UniProt
(Section~\ref{sec:related}). Less attention has gone to what happens
downstream when a pipeline looks atoms up in one scheme using positions given
in the other. Forensic re-analyses in high-throughput biology have found that
the most common errors are simple ones, such as row or column offsets
\citep{baggerly2009}. A shifted correspondence produces numbers that look
like results.

We measure the first problem in two corpora and two published evaluations,
and report an instance of the second from our own pipeline. Our contributions are the following.
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item A decomposition of exposure into training eligibility, template
      exposure and homology exposure, with a measurement for each. For AFDB
      models the template list can be read from the model file
      (Section~\ref{sec:channels}).
\item In ATLAS \citep{vandermeersche2024}, a molecular-dynamics corpus that
      has been reused to train and evaluate structure-ensemble generators
      \citep{jing2024alphaflow}, 791 of 868 proteins have only references
      released on or before AlphaFold2's training cutoff of 2018-04-30
      (Section~\ref{sec:atlas}).
\item Before any of its entries had been released, we preregistered a
      census of X-ray PDB entries released after 2026-09-11, collected in
      weekly batches: its design, code and predictions were fixed and
      timestamped. In its first four batches, covering releases up to
      2026-10-04, 87 of 138 proteins already had a structure of the same
      protein or a $\ge$95\%-identical one in the \emph{training-era PDB}
      (entries released on or before the training cutoff), and 45 of the 138
      AFDB models were given a same-protein template. In an earlier,
      exploratory census of entries released after 2022-06-02, proteins with
      such a relative had slightly higher lDDT, higher pLDDT by a larger
      margin and a share of \emph{confident errors} (residues with pLDDT
      $\ge$ 90 and lDDT $<$ 0.60) that we could not distinguish from that of
      other proteins (Section~\ref{sec:homology}).
\item In two published evaluations, at least 30 of the 82 test chains in
      the date-split ATLAS test set of AlphaFlow \citep{jing2024alphaflow}
      have a close relative in the training-era PDB (32 if matches to fusion
      partners are counted). In a template-free comparison with new crystal
      structures \citep{terwilliger2024}, high accuracy was also observed
      among structures with no detected relative, and we found no clear
      accuracy advantage associated with a close relative
      (Section~\ref{sec:published}).
\item In our census pipeline, a fallback that built the residue map in
      label numbering while looking atoms up by author numbering shifted the
      pairing of whole runs of residues and created residues with high pLDDT
      and low lDDT. A preregistered prediction about the coverage of
      conformal intervals for lDDT, which do not use pLDDT, failed on these
      values, and we withdraw that outcome as invalid. A rerun on corrected values, registered after the error was found and after a preliminary result was known, meets the registered decision rule at both nominal levels (0.90 and 0.95), and at 0.95 exceeds its threshold by only 0.0007. We give the mechanism, how often it is triggered and the checks
      that catch it (Section~\ref{sec:defect}, Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}).
\item A table of practical checks (Section~\ref{sec:recommendations}) and a
      public release of the registrations, code and derived tables.
\end{enumerate}
Sections~\ref{sec:related}--\ref{sec:setup} give related work, the exposure
channels and the methods, Sections~\ref{sec:atlas}--\ref{sec:defect} the
results, and Sections~\ref{sec:recommendations}--\ref{sec:limitations}
recommendations and limitations.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Leakage in machine learning for science.} Leakage is a recurring
cause of irreproducible results in machine-learning-based science
\citep{kaufman2012,kapoor2023}. Biology has its own guidance on validation
and data splitting \citep{walsh2021dome,whalen2022} and tools for splitting
biomolecular data by similarity \citep{joeres2025datasail}. In structural
biology, standard train--test splits leak through sequence and structural
similarity in protein--ligand affinity prediction
\citep{li2017jcim,volkov2022,li2024lppdbbind,graber2025}, and date-based or
metadata-based splits leave similar overlap in docking
\citep{buttenschoen2024} and protein--protein interaction benchmarks
\citep{bushuiev2024,kovtun2024pinder}. \citet{skrinjar2025} report that
protein--ligand co-folding methods ``largely memorize ligand poses from their
training data''.

\paragraph{Exposure in AlphaFold evaluation.} The AlphaFold2 paper evaluated
the model on PDB chains released after its training cutoff and examined
template similarity \citep{jumper2021}. BETA provides version-specific
benchmark sets with sequence and structure filters \citep{dobson2025beta}.
\citet{terwilliger2024} compared AlphaFold predictions made without PDB
templates with new crystal structures. AlphaFold's predictions of
fold-switched conformations have been traced to memorised training
structures \citep{chakravarty2024}, and retraining with parts of fold space
withheld shows that generalisation survives large omissions but varies with
what is withheld \citep{ahdritz2024}.

\paragraph{Residue correspondence.} The Worldwide PDB (wwPDB) remediated the
archive to address inconsistencies introduced over the years
\citep{henrick2008}. Mapping PDB chains to UniProt has long been non-trivial
\citep{martin2005}. SIFTS provides residue-level mappings between the two
\citep{velankar2013,dana2019}, records them in label numbering, and in
author numbering where residues were observed, and documents
the complications added by missing residues, insertions, expression tags and
linkers \citep{choudhary2023sifts}. Standard sequence alignments often
misalign the regions next to unobserved residues \citep{velankar2013}.
PDBrenum renumbers PDB files to match UniProt \citep{faezov2021pdbrenu}.
Where these resources address how to map residues correctly, we document
what one mapping mistake does to a downstream analysis.

\paragraph{Conformal prediction for structure.} Split conformal prediction
gives finite-sample marginal coverage under exchangeability
\citep{vovk2005,lei2018,angelopoulos2023}. When proteins are sampled, residues pooled across proteins are generally not exchangeable, and such hierarchical data need their own guarantees \citep{dunn2023,lee2023}, as do non-exchangeable
data more generally \citep{barber2023}. CalPro applies evidential and
conformal methods to pLDDT \citep{shihab2026calpro}. Our census
registration includes a split-conformal coverage test
(Section~\ref{sec:p5}); we do not re-evaluate CalPro.

\section{Exposure channels}
\label{sec:channels}

Let $R$ be an experimental reference structure, first released on date $r$,
compared with an AFDB model $M$ of the same protein. We distinguish three
channels through which $R$, or structures like it, could have informed $M$.

\paragraph{Training.} AlphaFold2 was trained on PDB structures with a
maximum release date of 2018-04-30 \citep{jumper2021}. If $r$ is on or
before that date, $R$ is \emph{training-eligible}: it may have been a
training example. Training also filtered chains and sampled them by cluster,
so eligibility is an upper bound on use of the PDB itself. Self-distillation
on predicted structures is a further route that release dates do not capture
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Templates.} At inference time, AlphaFold2 searches the PDB for
templates. The AlphaFold human-proteome study reports the snapshots it used
of the PDB and of PDB70, a clustered subset of the PDB used for template
search \citep{tunyasuvunakool2021}, and AFDB states that templates were
restricted to structures released before 2021-02-15 \citep{afdbfaq}. AFDB
model files record the PDB entries supplied as templates in the ModelCIF
category \texttt{\_ma\_\allowbreak template\_\allowbreak ref\_\allowbreak db\_\allowbreak details}
\citep{afdbfaq,vallat2023modelcif}, so this channel can be measured for each
model. The list records which templates the network was given, not how much
the prediction relied on them.

\paragraph{Homology.} Even when $R$ post-dates both cutoffs, the same
protein or a close relative may have been in the training-era PDB. Release
dates cannot capture this channel; we measure it by sequence similarity to
the training-era PDB \citep{jumper2021,buttenschoen2024}.

AFDB entries also carry a \texttt{modelCreatedDate}, documented as the date
the entry was created \citep{afdbmetadata}. In version~6, unchanged models
received new metadata and the v6 label while their coordinates were carried
over from version~4 \citep{pdbereleasenotes}. The creation date therefore
records neither when a prediction ran nor what it could see. We use it only
to exclude models created on or after the start of the preregistered census
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

\section{Data and methods}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Terms.} We use \emph{protein} for a UniProt accession, and call
two structures \emph{same-protein} if they map to the same accession. An
\emph{entry} is a PDB entry, which may contain several chains. A \emph{chain
segment} is the set of residues of one PDB chain mapped to one accession.
SIFTS describes it as one or more \emph{SIFTS segments}, each a contiguous
stretch with its own start and end. A residue is a \emph{confident error} if
its pLDDT is at least 90 and its lDDT below 0.60. At the chain level we use a
separate \emph{low-agreement screen}, which flags a chain segment of at least
30 residues with median pLDDT $\ge$ 90 and median lDDT $<$ 0.4. The
\emph{training-era PDB} is the set of entries released on or before
2018-04-30, and an \emph{accession--entry pair} is one protein in one PDB
entry. A residue is \emph{scored} if it has an lDDT value. Residue counts count each residue once per chain and entry, so a UniProt position observed in several chains is counted several times. A
\emph{registration} is an analysis plan, with its code and predictions,
fixed in a version-controlled repository, now public (Data and code availability), and timestamped
with OpenTimestamps
\citep{opentimestamps}, which anchors a file's SHA-256 hash in the Bitcoin
blockchain. We call an analysis \emph{preregistered} if it was registered
before any lDDT value it uses existed.

\paragraph{ATLAS.} The list of entries in the ATLAS download archive
\citep{atlasweb}, retrieved on 2026-08-30, has 1{,}938 entry--chain records
covering 1{,}735 PDB entries and 943 proteins, more than the datasets
described in the ATLAS paper \citep{vandermeersche2024}. On 2026-09-04 we
checked each record against PDBe and AFDB and kept chains that map to a
single UniProt accession through SIFTS \citep{velankar2013,dana2019}, with
the identity and coverage reported by PDBe at least 0.90 and 0.80, and whose
protein has an AFDB model. Four of the 1{,}938 records could not be checked because of network errors. The kept chains cover 869 proteins. One has an AFDB record
we could not parse, which leaves 868 proteins and 935 accession--entry
pairs. For each reference we took the initial release date,
the first record in the mmCIF category
\texttt{\_pdbx\_audit\_revision\_history} \citep{mmcifdict}.

\paragraph{Preregistered census.} To obtain references released after both cutoffs of Section~\ref{sec:channels}, we preregistered a census: every X-ray PDB entry at
2.5\,\AA{} resolution or better released after $T_0$ = 2026-09-11 00:00 UTC,
collected in weekly batches. Models come from AFDB version~6. A complete AFDB version~6 index was outside the registered access and rate-limit constraints, so the registered design builds its list of models incrementally: a protein enters the list when it first appears in the census, its model is accepted only if its \texttt{modelCreatedDate} falls before the day of $T_0$, and the model file is downloaded when its batch is processed. This is an operational rule based on metadata dates, which do not record when a prediction ran (Section~\ref{sec:channels}). The design, analysis code and predictions were registered on
2026-09-09, and two dated amendments were made before any data were
collected: one shortened the first batch to 48 hours and the other fixed the
code that starts each batch. Apart from the release-date filter, which
places every reference after AFDB's 2021-02-15 template cutoff, the design
adds no exposure control. What it adds is that every choice was fixed before
any lDDT value was computed. The residue mapping was replaced later, after
the error described in Section~\ref{sec:defect}. We report the first four of
12 planned batches, covering releases up to 2026-10-04. The first, 48-hour
batch contained no weekly PDB release day and is empty. With the original
mapping the four batches gave 488 chain segments from 272 accession--entry
pairs and 138 proteins, and with the corrected mapping 495 chain segments
and 141 proteins; Table~\ref{tab:scope} in Section~\ref{sec:scope} shows how the batches narrow from enumerated entries to scored proteins. Most excluded chain mappings failed the registered AFDB version~6 admission checks (no eligible model found, metadata not retrievable, or a model file that was not version~6). The exposure measurements of Section~\ref{sec:homology} were made on the 138 proteins of the original set. The measurement for a given protein needs no residue pairing, but the error did decide which proteins were scored: the corrected mapping adds three (Section~\ref{sec:scope}). All lDDT and coverage results use the corrected set unless stated otherwise.



\paragraph{Post-2022 census.} An earlier census, assembled before the
preregistered one and not preregistered, covers 168 X-ray entries at
2.5\,\AA{} or better released after 2022-06-02 and after the creation date
recorded for their AFDB model. Its residue mapping used the same numbering
fallback but discarded pairs whose amino acids differed, and it was affected
by the same error. It scored chain~A of each entry only. Its candidates were the X-ray entries at 2.5\,\AA{} or better released after 2022-06-02 (33{,}517 in an RCSB search on 2026-09-05). The step that reduced them to 168, which included a filter on entry size (fewer than 5{,}000 atoms), is not part of the released code; only its resulting list is released. We use it for the exposure comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:effect} and to check how the error behaves under a per-pair
identity filter (Section~\ref{sec:post2022}). With the corrected mapping, 139
of its entries from 85 proteins are scored (96 entries from 64 proteins
before). All its results outside Section~\ref{sec:post2022} use the
corrected lDDT values.

\paragraph{Model versions.} The cutoffs of Section~\ref{sec:channels} are
those of AlphaFold2, but AFDB also serves ColabFold models
\citep{mirdita2022}, so we checked the software recorded in each model file.
All 138 model files of the preregistered census's original exposure roster are AlphaFold2 models
(``AlphaFold Monomer v2.0''). Of the 93 model files downloaded for the
post-2022 census, 6 are ColabFold Monomer v1.5.2 models and 87 are AlphaFold
Monomer v2.0, and none of the six belongs to a protein in the exposure
comparison of Section~\ref{sec:effect}.

\paragraph{Residue pairing and lDDT.} For each reference chain we pair
C$\alpha$ atoms with AFDB model residues through the PDBe SIFTS UniProt
mapping. We describe the corrected mapping here, and
Section~\ref{sec:defect} the original and its error. The corrected code
(i) reads both the SIFTS positions and the atoms in label numbering
(\texttt{label\_seq\_id}), with chains identified by their label identifier
(\texttt{label\_asym\_id}, called \texttt{struct\_asym\_id} in the SIFTS
files), so that no lookup crosses numbering schemes; (ii) maps a SIFTS segment whose PDB and UniProt ranges have equal length by a constant offset, drops any pair whose amino acids differ, and rejects the segment if more than 10\% of its pairs differ; and (iii) otherwise aligns the deposited sequence within the segment's label range to the AFDB model sequence within its UniProt range (global alignment; match $+2$, mismatch $-1$, gap $-2$; ties favour pairing over a gap) and keeps only identical aligned pairs, so the 10\% rule cannot reject these segments. Segments with fewer than 30 paired residues are excluded, and lDDT is computed per SIFTS segment; in the four batches every scored chain segment consists of a single SIFTS segment, so this is the same as computing it per chain segment. We use C$\alpha$ atoms that have no alternative location or are at location A, and exclude modified residues other than selenomethionine; in the four
batches with the corrected mapping these two rules remove 16 and 57
residues. We score each residue by lDDT-C$\alpha$ \citep{mariani2013},
computed against the AFDB model over paired residues with
a 15\,\AA{} inclusion radius and distance-difference thresholds of 0.5, 1, 2
and 4\,\AA. pLDDT is read from the model's B-factor column and is never used
in computing lDDT.

\paragraph{Homology exposure.} For each census protein we queried the search
service of the RCSB PDB (Research Collaboratory for Structural
Bioinformatics; \citealp{burley2023}) for training-era entries in three
classes: (i)~entries mapped to the same UniProt accession, (ii)~entries containing a
chain of a different accession at $\ge$95\% sequence identity and
(iii)~entries containing one at $\ge$30\% identity. Classes (ii) and (iii)
come from MMseqs2 sequence searches \citep{steinegger2017} with an E-value of at most 0.1 and the canonical
UniProt sequence as query; identity is measured over the aligned region the search returns. We set no coverage threshold, so a hit of any class, even a same-protein hit, may cover only part of the protein, and the absence of a hit does not rule out remote homology. Each protein is
assigned its strongest class. A failed query would leave a protein
unresolved rather than novel, but none failed: for the 224 proteins queried
(138 from the preregistered census and 86 from the post-2022 census, namely the proteins scored there before or after correction; one of them, Q2G0L4, is not among the 85 scored after correction because the identity checks reject both its entries), 594
queries returned hits, two of them repeats of an identical query, and 408
returned none \citep{rcsbsearchapi}.

\paragraph{Template exposure.} From each AFDB model file we read the
template PDB IDs, mapped each template to UniProt through SIFTS and recorded
its release date. A model has a \emph{same-protein template} if any template entry contains a chain that maps to its accession. For 44 of the 45 such models in the preregistered census, the template chain named in the model file itself maps to the accession. For the remaining one (Q5UE59, template 6SWU), the model file names the chains AAA and FFF while SIFTS names them A to F; every chain of 6SWU maps to Q5UE59. It is \emph{unresolved} if none does and at least one
template could not be mapped.

\paragraph{Preregistered coverage prediction.} The census registration also
predicts the coverage of split-conformal intervals for residue lDDT that are
built without pLDDT, so the prediction is sensitive to the lDDT values and the pipeline rather than to pLDDT; we call its evaluation the coverage test. Section~\ref{sec:p5} describes it with its result.

\section{Most ATLAS references predate the training cutoff}
\label{sec:atlas}

For ATLAS we measured only the training channel. We count a protein as
training-eligible when all of its retained references were released on or before
2018-04-30. Of 868 ATLAS proteins, 791 (91.1\%) are training-eligible in
this sense, and so are 851 of the 935 accession--entry pairs (91.0\%). The
references were released between 1988 and 2023 (median release year 2008
over the 935 pairs). Only 77
proteins have any reference released after the cutoff, so agreement between
AFDB models and ATLAS references cannot by itself be read as evidence of
generalisation. Comparing each reference's release date with its model's
\texttt{modelCreatedDate} instead, a tempting but wrong proxy
(Section~\ref{sec:channels}), would make all references of 863 of 868
proteins (99.4\%) predate their model.

ATLAS was built to describe dynamics, and reusing it to study AlphaFold
error requires an explicit exposure control. The obvious control is to keep
only references released after the cutoff. Section~\ref{sec:homology} asks,
on a separate set of newly released structures, how much that achieves, and
Section~\ref{sec:published} applies the same question to a published ATLAS
test split.

the post-2022 census, whether exposure goes with higher agreement
(Section~\ref{sec:effect}), and measures homology exposure in two published
evaluations (Section~\ref{sec:published}).

\subsection{Homology exposure}
\label{sec:homexp}

Every structure in the preregistered census was released more than eight years after the
training cutoff, yet 87 of the 138 proteins (63\%) had a same-protein or
near-identical ($\ge$95\% identity) structure in the training-era PDB, 128
(93\%) had a relative at $\ge$30\% identity, and only 10 (7\%) had none
(Table~\ref{tab:homology}). For same-protein hits, the earlier structure may
cover only part of the protein. Some well-studied proteins have hundreds of
such structures: hen egg-white lysozyme (P00698) had 730 same-protein
entries in the training-era PDB, human carbonic anhydrase~2 (P00918) had 700
and $\beta_2$-microglobulin (P61769) had 693. These hits show only that a
related structure existed, not how it differs, for example by ligand,
mutation, construct or crystal form.

\subsection{Template exposure}
\label{sec:tmplexp}

The template lists give a more direct measure. All 138 model files list four
PDB templates each, released between 1988 and January 2021, so none
post-dates the documented 2021-02-15 cutoff. For 45 of the 138 models
(33\%) at least one template is a structure of the same protein, for 88 all
templates are of other proteins, and 5 are unresolved (Table~\ref{tab:homology}). The
homology and template channels overlap only in part. Of the 78 proteins with
a same-protein structure in the training-era PDB, 43 were given a
same-protein template and 35 were not; none of the 35 is unresolved. Conversely, two proteins with no
same-protein structure in the training-era PDB were given a same-protein
template (released in 2019 and 2020). One of them, Q9F0J8, has no
training-era relative at $\ge$30\% identity, so a homology screen alone
would call it novel although its model was given a structure of the same
protein as a template.

\subsection{Is exposure associated with agreement?}
\label{sec:effect}

This comparison uses the post-2022 census, for a reason given at the end
of this section. We compared its proteins with and without a \emph{close
relative} in the training-era PDB (a same-protein structure or one at
$\ge$95\% identity), using lDDT values recomputed with the corrected mapping
(Section~\ref{sec:post2022}). We fixed the comparison in a timestamped plan
before running it, but after its lDDT values existed, so it is exploratory.
For each protein we took the median lDDT and the median pLDDT over its
residues, and for each group we then averaged these per-protein medians.
Intervals are 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals from 10{,}000 resamples of
proteins within each group. Of the 85 scored proteins, the 40 with a close
relative had a mean median lDDT of 0.975, against 0.959 for the 45 without one, of which 38 have a relative at at least 30\% but below 95\% identity and 7 have none found (difference 0.016, interval 0.003--0.032). Their mean median pLDDT was
also higher, 96.1 against 92.3 (difference 3.8, interval 1.7--6.0), a larger
gap than in lDDT (3.8 against 1.6 points on a 0--100 scale), although we did
not test the difference between the two gaps formally. The mean per-protein share of confident errors, counted over all scored residues of each protein, was 0.12\% with a close relative and 0.13\% without
(difference $-0.01$ percentage points, interval $-0.18$ to $0.17$). The
interval is wider than either share, so the data cannot tell the groups
apart on this measure. These are associations in a small sample: proteins with close relatives may also differ in size, family and how often they have been studied. We did not run this comparison on the preregistered
census because its registration also makes predictions about coverage within
pLDDT bins that are not yet evaluated, and comparing pLDDT with lDDT there
first would compromise their blindness.

\subsection{Two published evaluations}
\label{sec:published}

To see how common homology exposure is outside our own censuses, and
whether it goes with accuracy, we measured it in two published evaluations
that use structures released after a training cutoff. The plan was registered
and timestamped before any exposure query was sent; it discloses that one
summary of each data set (an overall median and a count of release dates) had
already been looked at. It uses the classes of Section~\ref{sec:setup}, except
that the query is the evaluated chain's own sequence rather than the
canonical UniProt sequence. As in Section~\ref{sec:effect}, a \emph{close
relative} is a same-protein structure or one at $\ge$95\% identity in the
training-era PDB. An independent recomputation with separately written code
reproduced the counts and medians below and, with different random seeds,
the statistical conclusion.

\paragraph{Template-free predictions of new crystal structures.}
\citet{terwilliger2024} compared AlphaFold predictions made without templates
with crystal structures released between December 2021 and May 2022, using
the first chain of each entry. They report a median C$\alpha$ root-mean-square
deviation (r.m.s.d.) of 1.0\,\AA{} between prediction and deposited model,
lower than the 2.3\,\AA{} of DeepMind's own analysis of recent structures,
``perhaps due to the high confidence in prediction in our sample''. Their text
describes this analysis as using all 215 structures of their earlier study; in
the workbook released with the paper, the reported median is 0.954\,\AA{},
computed over a list of 102 structures with a free R value of 0.30 or lower.
We used that list. As a proxy for the authors' first chain we took the
protein entity with the lowest identifier. One entry, 7DRH, has since been
removed from the PDB and could not be classed. Of the other 101 structures,
24 have a close relative. Their median r.m.s.d.\ was 0.847\,\AA{}, against
0.956\,\AA{} for the other 77. The difference, $-0.11$\,\AA{} (95\% bootstrap
interval $-0.41$ to 0.53; permutation $p = 0.38$), does not meet our
registered criterion, an interval entirely below zero, and the comparison has
little power. The 49 structures with no relative found at $\ge$30\% identity
had a median of 0.943\,\AA{}. High accuracy was therefore also observed among
structures with no detected training-era relative, and we found no clear
accuracy advantage associated with a close relative. This observational
comparison cannot rule out an effect of exposure, and it does not test the
authors' explanation, but it gives no sign that their low r.m.s.d.\ depends
on exposure.

\paragraph{A date-split test set for ensemble generators.} AlphaFlow and
ESMFlow \citep{jing2024alphaflow} are fine-tuned from AlphaFold2 and ESMFold
and then on ATLAS (Section~\ref{sec:atlas}). Their ATLAS split is by date,
with training and validation cutoffs of 2018-05-01 and 2019-05-01. Its test
set holds the 82 chains whose entries were deposited after 2019-05-01 (two
longer chains excluded), and the paper describes these test proteins as
having ``minimal structural overlap with the training ensembles, providing a
stringent test of generalization''. The split uses no homology filter. In the
released split files, 32 of the 82 test chains (39\%) have a close relative
in the training-era PDB, 28 a structure of the same protein and 4 one at
$\ge$95\% identity. A further 25 have a relative at $\ge$30\% identity, and 25
have none found. Three of the 32 are fusion constructs, which carry two
UniProt accessions, and the registered rule counts a match to either. For
two of them, a fusion with maltose-binding protein and one with T4 lysozyme,
the close match is a structure of the fusion partner. Thirty test chains
(37\%) therefore have a close match supported by the protein of interest;
the registered, fusion-inclusive count is 32. This range is a post hoc
sensitivity bound: we did not rerun the sequence searches on the proteins of
interest alone. Against AlphaFlow's own training split (1{,}266 chains in the
released file; the paper gives 1{,}265), five test chains have a relative at
$\ge$95\% identity and ten one at $\ge$30\%. Three of the five matches go
through a fusion partner. The other two are a structure of the same protein
and, for the SARS-CoV-2 main protease, the main protease of SARS-CoV, which is
at least 95\% identical. Near-identical sequence overlap with the MD training
split is therefore small, in line with the paper's description, whereas close
relatives in the training-era PDB are common. In addition, 40 of the 82 test
entries were released on or before 2020-05-01, the ESMFold training cutoff
stated by \citet{jing2024alphaflow}, and so are training-eligible for
ESMFold. These counts describe the split. We did not analyse per-target
results and make no claim that the reported performance is inflated.

\section{A residue-correspondence error created spurious confident errors}
\label{sec:defect}

The exposure measurements need no residue pairing, but any comparison of
agreement does. It needs per-residue lDDT values, and computing them for the
census is where our own pipeline failed. After the fourth batch the
preregistered coverage prediction, which does not use pLDDT, failed at both
nominal levels, 0.90 and 0.95 (Section~\ref{sec:p5}). A post hoc check
found that one protein, K7PQ54, held 14\% of the scored residues and about
half of the residues that fell outside the 90\% intervals, counted as test-set occurrences pooled over the 200 splits. Tracing it led to the numbering fallback, an error that makes the model look worse than it is, whereas exposure can make it look better. The registration and the stored
source files let us confirm the cause and rescore the census.

\subsection{Mechanism}
\label{sec:mechanism}

In the segment-level PDBe SIFTS mapping files we used (PDBe also distributes
per-residue mappings), each SIFTS segment has a start and
an end in label numbering and, when the boundary residue was observed, in
author numbering. Our census code built each SIFTS segment's residue map
from its starting author number and fell back to the starting label number
when the author number was missing, which happens whenever the first residue
of the segment is unobserved. Our code then looked atoms up by author
number. In a segment where the fallback was used and author numbers
differed from label numbers by a constant offset, every residue was paired
with a UniProt position displaced by that offset, a \emph{register shift}.
The code had no amino-acid identity check to catch it.

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} shows chain~A of entry 9qj6, a structure of
K7PQ54, dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase from the lesser grain borer
\emph{Rhyzopertha dominica} (507 residues; \citealp{uniprot2025}). The SIFTS
segment starts at label residue~1 (UniProt~35), which is unobserved. Author
numbering runs 34 ahead of label numbering, so the atom with author
number~36 (label~2, UniProt~36) was assigned UniProt~70. Under this shift
chain~A had a median lDDT of 0.198 at a median pLDDT of 98.4, and chain~B,
shifted in the same way, had 0.199. With the mapping fixed, chains~A and~B
paired 472 and 471 residues with no sequence mismatch and had median
lDDT 0.993 and 0.995.

\subsection{The error mimics overconfidence, and a SIFTS-segment identity check catches it}
\label{sec:detect}

The error created residues with high pLDDT and very low lDDT, the pattern an
overconfident model would produce. A calibration study run on these values
could have concluded that AlphaFold2 is overconfident on new structures, and
our preregistered coverage test, which does not use pLDDT, also failed on
them (Section~\ref{sec:p5}).

The fallback is triggered by a SIFTS segment that begins on an unobserved
residue, and this is common: 304 of the 492 SIFTS segments mapped to the 138 original proteins
(62\%) begin this way. It causes a shift when author and label numbering are offset at the start of the segment, and an offset that changes partway along a segment causes further shifts. A nonzero offset at the segment start occurred in 147 of the 492 SIFTS segments (30\%), from 39 proteins, with offsets (author minus label number) from $-31$
to $+201$. In a post hoc comparison, all 147 lay within the
151 chain segments whose median lDDT changed by more than 0.2. Each of the
other four of those 151 contains a SIFTS segment whose offset changes
partway along it.

Checking amino-acid identity for every pair is necessary but not
sufficient. When we applied 18 register shifts to four corrected chains, only 5--7\% of pairs matched by chance, and such pairs pass a per-pair filter.
What catches the shift is the per-pair check together with rejecting any
SIFTS segment in which more than 10\% of pairs differ. These checks are diagnostics, not guarantees: a pair can pass them and still sit one position off in an ambiguous alignment, as in chain~A of 21xg (Section~\ref{sec:scope}). The post-2022 census
had only the per-pair check. It therefore lost most shifted residues but
kept those that matched by chance (Section~\ref{sec:post2022}). Coarser checks raise alerts, though real conformational differences can also trigger them. A set in which 31.9\% of residues fall below 0.60
lDDT is far from the 2.6\% in a separate set, the corrected post-2022
census, and 13 entries had chains that differed by 0.4 or more in median
lDDT. A chain-consistency check, which compares median lDDT across an
entry's chains, would still miss entries such as 9qj6, where both
chains were shifted alike.

\subsection{The same error in the post-2022 census}
\label{sec:post2022}

Of the 168 entries of the post-2022 census, 96 had originally been scored,
69 had been rejected for having too few paired residues and 3 because the
model could not be downloaded. With the corrected mapping, 45 of those 69
entries yield lDDT values. In all, 139 entries are scored instead of 96
(37{,}531 scored residues instead of 24{,}457). The total is 96 + 45 $-$ 2 because two previously scored
entries, 10pa and 10ps, are now rejected by the identity checks. The
Spearman rank correlation between pLDDT and lDDT rose from 0.617 to 0.643.
The pooled residue-level share of confident errors, taken over all residues
with pLDDT $\ge$ 90, halved from 0.58\% (121 of 20{,}777) to 0.29\% (90 of 31{,}032); unlike the per-protein shares of Section~\ref{sec:effect}, its denominator is residues with pLDDT $\ge$ 90. In the lowest-confidence bin (pLDDT $<$ 50) the share
below 0.60 lDDT rose from 58\% ($n = 177$) to 75\% ($n = 314$).

The added residues and the rise in the lowest-confidence bin come from the
45 newly scored entries. Most of the rise in rank correlation, and more
than half of the fall in confident errors, come from recomputing entries
that were already scored, where shifted residues had matched by chance and
passed the per-pair filter. Entry 10ke, for example, held 22 of the 121 original confident errors. The
input files of the original post-2022 run, downloaded in September 2026, had
not been kept, so this recomputation used files downloaded again in October
2026; changes to those files between the two downloads could account for
part of the difference.

\subsection{The preregistered coverage prediction before and after correction}
\label{sec:p5}

The census registration includes a prediction that is sensitive to the pipeline rather than to AlphaFold2's confidence. Let $y$ be a residue's lDDT and $1-\alpha$ the
nominal coverage. For each of 200 random protein-level splits (seeds
0--199), 50\% of proteins form a fitting set, 25\% a calibration set and
25\% a test set. The fitting set serves only to compute $\hat y$, a single
number: the median lDDT over its residues. Every test residue receives the
interval $\hat y \pm q$, where $q$ is the
$\lceil (n_{\mathrm{cal}}+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$-th smallest of the calibration
scores $|y-\hat y|$ and $n_{\mathrm{cal}}$ is the number of calibration
residues \citep{vovk2005,lei2018}. At $1-\alpha = 0.90$ and $0.95$, we
predicted that mean residue-level test coverage over the 200 splits would be
at least the nominal level minus three Monte Carlo standard errors (MCSE,
the standard deviation of coverage across splits divided by $\sqrt{200}$),
a threshold that depends on the data. Residues enter the test if their stored lDDT, pLDDT, Kabsch distance and B-factor z-score are finite and $0 \le$ pLDDT $< 100.01$; pLDDT is not an input to the interval or the nonconformity score. The MCSE measures variation over random splits of these proteins, not uncertainty about new proteins. Calibration
residues are pooled across proteins of different sizes, and for such
hierarchical data marginal residue-level coverage carries no finite-sample
guarantee \citep{dunn2023,lee2023}.

On the original lDDT values the prediction failed at both levels
(Table~\ref{tab:p5}). Because those values were corrupted by the numbering fallback, we withdraw this outcome as invalid; a dated, timestamped correction
notice in the repository states this and why (Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}).
The original outputs remain archived unchanged. On the corrected values
(123{,}454 residues from 141 proteins) both means are slightly below nominal but pass their thresholds. Meeting this rule shows neither that coverage is nominal nor that the lDDT values are right: values all rescaled by the same positive factor and shift would give the same coverage in exact arithmetic. At 0.90 the mean exceeds the threshold by 0.0031,
about one MCSE. At 0.95 it exceeds it by 0.0007, less than half an MCSE
(0.0017), so this level passes narrowly and could reverse under a different
but equally defensible correction of the residue mapping. lDDT takes a limited set of values and is stored to four decimals, so some test residues lie exactly on an interval end
$\hat y \pm q$; counting them as uncovered leaves the pass intact (0.9453
against 0.9449). The original thresholds were lower because the original
MCSE (0.0080 at 0.90, 0.0057 at 0.95) was more than twice the corrected one:
K7PQ54 alone held 14\% of the original residues, so a split's coverage
depended strongly on whether K7PQ54 fell in its test set.

The seeds and the decision rule were fixed by the original registration, and
the corrected mapping and the protocol for rerunning the test on corrected
values were registered before the rerun. The rerun is not blind. Before
registering it, we computed the same statistic outside the registered
pipeline on an early version of the corrected mapping (122{,}024 residues
rather than 123{,}454) and obtained 0.898 and 0.947. The rerun used the
registered evaluation code unchanged, and no new batches were collected
until it finished. Two reimplementations written from the registration,
without importing the evaluation code, reproduced both means exactly, and
one also reproduced the original failure. The corrected pass, together with
the mechanism in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}, makes the original failure
consistent with an artefact of the residue mapping. Because the interval ignores pLDDT, the result says nothing about how well pLDDT tracks error; what it showed is that the test was sensitive to this mapping error in this census.

Table~\ref{tab:checks} lists checks for the two problems reported here, with
the evidence for each. We recommend the following.
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item Report exposure by channel, and state which channels the question
      requires to be controlled. Release dates alone measure only training
      eligibility, and AFDB creation dates measure none of the channels
      (Section~\ref{sec:atlas}).
\item For AFDB models, read the template list from the model file
      (Section~\ref{sec:homology}).
\item Stratify results by sequence identity to the training-era PDB
      \citep{buttenschoen2024}, or split data by similarity
      \citep{joeres2025datasail} (Sections~\ref{sec:homology}
      and~\ref{sec:published}). When the query is a chain, check fusion
      constructs: a match to the fusion partner is not a relative of the
      protein of interest.
\item Pair residues in one numbering scheme, preferably from per-residue mappings where available, check amino-acid identity for every pair, reject any SIFTS segment with more than 10\% mismatches,
      and run the coarser checks of Table~\ref{tab:checks} before any
      downstream analysis (Section~\ref{sec:detect}).
\item Before attributing a failed calibration or coverage test to the model,
      rule out errors in the reference values. Our failed coverage test,
      which does not use pLDDT, is consistent with an error in the lDDT values
      (Section~\ref{sec:p5}).
\item Register analyses before lDDT values exist and keep the raw inputs
      (Sections~\ref{sec:scope} and~\ref{sec:post2022}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Our training channel uses AlphaFold2's 2018-04-30 cutoff. Other versions,
including AlphaFold3 \citep{abramson2024}, have their own cutoffs, and our
shares do not transfer to them. We did not measure exposure through multiple
sequence alignments or self-distillation, and template lists do not show how
much a prediction relied on its templates (Section~\ref{sec:channels}). Homology was measured with fixed identity
thresholds, a single canonical sequence and no coverage threshold. A
$\ge$30\% hit can be a short domain match, which overstates homology
exposure, and a single canonical sequence can miss isoform-specific hits,
which understates it. lDDT compares one experimental chain with a
monomer model, so partner chains, ligands or crystal packing can change the
experimental conformation and lower lDDT for reasons unrelated to
exposure. We score C$\alpha$ lDDT, so our values are
not directly comparable with all-atom lDDT.

The preregistered census samples new PDB entries, which over-represent proteins that are
crystallised repeatedly, so its exposure shares describe what a post-cutoff
release filter yields rather than the novelty of proteins in general. It is
reported after four of its 12 batches, and the full census may differ.
Because the correction adds and drops residues, the before-and-after
coverage comparison is not made on identical residues
(Section~\ref{sec:scope}), and because the correction changes which proteins are scored, the same seeds do not give the same splits, so the two results are not a paired comparison. The corrected coverage result comes from a rerun
registered after a preliminary result was known, and it passes at 0.95 by
less than half an MCSE (Section~\ref{sec:p5}). The numbering fallback is documented in one pipeline, ours. The trigger rates we report (Section~\ref{sec:detect}) describe this census, and
we have not examined whether any published analysis is affected by it. For the two published evaluations of Section~\ref{sec:published} we measured homology exposure only, apart from counting entries released before ESMFold's cutoff; we used a proxy for the first chain of each Terwilliger et al.\ entry; the registered rule classes a fusion chain by any of its accessions, and the 30-chain lower bound is post hoc; and the r.m.s.d.\ comparison has little power and is observational. The post-2022 census scored chain~A only, and the step that selected its 168 entries is not released. The census model list rests on metadata dates (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). The bootstrap intervals of Section~\ref{sec:effect} resample proteins and ignore relatedness between them. Finally, the three exposure channels overlap and do not cover every route (Section~\ref{sec:channels}).

\section{Conclusion}

Agreement between an AFDB model and an experimental structure is evidence about AlphaFold's generalisation to unexposed proteins only if two things are known: what the model could have
seen, and that the residues being compared are the same residues. In ATLAS,
91\% of proteins had only training-eligible references. In the preregistered
census, whose references all post-date the training cutoff, 63\% of proteins had a
same-protein or $\ge$95\%-identical structure in the training-era PDB. In an
exploratory comparison on an earlier census, proteins with such a relative
had slightly higher lDDT and higher pLDDT. In a published date-split test set,
at least 30 of 82 test chains had such a relative, while in a published
template-free comparison we found no clear accuracy advantage with one. In our own pipeline a numbering fallback, with no
SIFTS-segment identity check to catch it, made accurate predictions look
like confident errors. A preregistered coverage prediction, which does not
use pLDDT, failed on the resulting values; a rerun registered after the
error and a preliminary result were known meets it on corrected values,
narrowly at the 0.95 level. Registration, stored
inputs and the checks in Table~\ref{tab:checks} are cheap enough to be
routine for AFDB comparisons.

\section*{Data and code availability}

The registrations with their timestamp proofs, the analysis code, the
derived residue-level tables, the SHA-256 hashes of the downloaded files and
web responses, and the reports of the independent checks are available at
\url{https://github.com/MachineLearning-Nerd/afdb-exposure-audit}. A script in the repository runs 236 checks: 168 recompute numbers in this paper from the row-level tables and cached responses, 45 check stored outputs against those recomputations, and 23 compare numbers in the paper with stored outputs. It checks numbers, not the prose. The census's other registered predictions have not been evaluated; its evaluation outputs, which contain fields for them, are withheld until the census closes. Raw files from the PDB, AFDB, PDBe/SIFTS,
UniProt and ATLAS are not redistributed but can be re-fetched, and the
recorded hashes identify the exact files used. The ATLAS counts in
Sections~\ref{sec:setup} and~\ref{sec:atlas} are computed from released
tables. One intermediate step, which sorted the 869 proteins into two lists for a separate, unpublished study (this paper uses both lists), reads a file that is not released, so that step cannot be rerun; its outputs are included. Likewise, the step that selected the 168 entries of the post-2022 census is not released; the resulting list is. For Section~\ref{sec:published}, the registration, code, per-chain classes and AlphaFlow split files are released; the workbook of \citet{terwilliger2024} is not redistributed, but its source and hash are recorded.
