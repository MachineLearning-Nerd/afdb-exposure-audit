\begin{abstract}
Comparisons of AlphaFold Database (AFDB) models with experimental structures
underpin knowledge of pLDDT, AlphaFold's prediction of the local
distance difference test (lDDT). Two problems can distort them silently:
data leakage, when the model has seen the reference or a close relative, and
residues paired with the wrong reference atoms. In the ATLAS
molecular-dynamics corpus, 791 of 868 proteins (91\%) had only references
dated on or before AlphaFold2's training cutoff. In the first four batches
of a preregistered census of structures released after 2026-09-11, 87 of 138
proteins (63\%) had a same-protein or $\ge$95\%-identical structure from
before the cutoff, 10 (7\%) had no relative at $\ge$30\% identity, and 45
models (33\%) were given a same-protein template. In an exploratory
comparison on an earlier census, proteins with close relatives had slightly
higher lDDT, higher pLDDT by a larger margin and similar shares of confident
errors. The second problem arose in our pipeline, which applied PDBe/SIFTS
\texttt{label\_seq\_id} positions to atoms indexed by
\texttt{auth\_seq\_id}. Correcting it changed the median lDDT of 188 of 488
chain segments (151 by more than 0.2) and removed spurious confident errors
mimicking AlphaFold overconfidence. A preregistered coverage prediction that
tests the lDDT values and pipeline, not pLDDT, failed on the original
values; we withdraw that recorded outcome as invalid. A re-evaluation
registered after the error was found and a preliminary result was known
meets it: mean coverage 0.8945 and 0.9456, slightly below nominal but above
the thresholds (nominal minus three Monte Carlo standard errors) of 0.8914
and 0.9449.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

AlphaFold2 \citep{jumper2021} and the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database
(AFDB; \citealp{varadi2022,bertoni2026}) have made predicted structures
available for hundreds of millions of proteins. For each residue, AlphaFold
reports a confidence score that predicts the local distance difference test
(lDDT), a superposition-free score of local agreement between a model and a
reference structure \citep{mariani2013}. The score is called predicted lDDT
(pLDDT), and much of what is known about it comes from comparing AFDB models
with other data on the same protein. Comparisons with experimental
structures, simulations and disorder annotations ask how well pLDDT tracks
local error \citep{jumper2021}, whether it reflects flexibility or disorder
\citep{akdel2022,ruff2021,guo2022,saldano2022,carugo2023}, and whether
conformal intervals built on pLDDT reach their nominal coverage
\citep{shihab2026calpro}. Two problems can make such comparisons misleading
without stopping the pipeline. Agreement can look too good because the model
has seen the reference or something close to it. It can look too bad because
model residues were paired with the wrong reference atoms.

The first problem is \emph{exposure}, any route by which the reference or a
close relative could have informed the model: as a training example, as a
\emph{template} (a known structure given to the network as input at
prediction time), or as a close homologue in the training data. For some
questions, such as describing AFDB as it is used, exposure does not matter.
For others, such as accuracy on new proteins or the calibration of pLDDT on
unseen folds, agreement with an exposed reference may reflect recall rather
than generalisation. This is data leakage \citep{kaufman2012,kapoor2023},
and we call the routes by which it can enter an AFDB comparison
\emph{exposure channels}. Structure prediction has long guarded against
leakage through blind and time-split evaluation
\citep{kryshtafovych2021,haas2018,jumper2021}, and recent benchmarks show
that release-date splits alone still leak through homology
\citep{buttenschoen2024,durairaj2024,dobson2025beta,bushuiev2024}. In a
retrospective AFDB comparison the model is fixed and part of what it saw is
recorded in its model file, so exposure can be measured rather than assumed.

The second problem is \emph{residue correspondence}. Model residues are
numbered by their position in the UniProt sequence. Reference atoms are
numbered within an entry of the Protein Data Bank (PDB), whose mmCIF
(macromolecular Crystallographic Information File) format carries two
schemes: a sequential \emph{label numbering} (\texttt{label\_seq\_id}) and an
\emph{author numbering} (\texttt{auth\_seq\_id}) chosen by the depositors
\citep{westbrook2022,rcsbidentifiers,mmcifdict}. Resources such as SIFTS
(Structure Integration with Function, Taxonomy and Sequences), distributed by
the Protein Data Bank in Europe (PDBe), map these numberings to UniProt
(Section~\ref{sec:related}). Less attention has gone to what happens
downstream when a pipeline looks atoms up in one scheme using positions given
in the other. Forensic re-analyses in high-throughput biology have found that
the most common errors are simple ones, such as row or column offsets
\citep{baggerly2009}. A shifted correspondence produces numbers that look
like results.

We measure the first problem in two corpora and report an instance of the
second from our own pipeline. Our contributions are the following.
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item A decomposition of exposure into training eligibility, template
      exposure and homology exposure, with a measurement for each. For AFDB
      models the template list can be read from the model file
      (Section~\ref{sec:channels}).
\item In ATLAS \citep{vandermeersche2024}, a molecular-dynamics corpus that
      has been reused to train and evaluate structure-ensemble generators
      \citep{jing2024alphaflow}, 791 of 868 proteins have only references
      released on or before AlphaFold2's training cutoff
      (Section~\ref{sec:atlas}).
\item We registered a census of PDB entries released after 2026-09-11
      before any data existed. In its first four batches (an empty 48-hour
      batch followed by three weekly ones), 87 of 138 proteins already had a
      structure of the same protein or a $\ge$95\%-identical one in the
      \emph{training-era PDB} (entries released on or before the 2018-04-30
      cutoff), and 45 of the 138 AFDB models were given a same-protein
      template. In an earlier census, proteins with a close relative had
      slightly higher lDDT, higher pLDDT by a larger margin and a similar
      share of confident errors (Section~\ref{sec:homology}).
\item In our census pipeline, a fallback between the two numbering schemes
      shifted the pairing of whole runs of residues and created residues
      with high pLDDT and low lDDT. A preregistered coverage prediction,
      which does not use pLDDT, failed on these values, and we withdraw that
      recorded outcome as invalid. A re-evaluation registered after the
      error was found, and after a preliminary result was known, meets the
      prediction on corrected values, at the 0.95 level by a margin of
      0.0007. We give the mechanism, how often it is triggered and the checks
      that catch it (Section~\ref{sec:defect}, Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}).
\item A table of practical checks (Section~\ref{sec:recommendations}) and a
      public release of the registrations, code and derived tables.
\end{enumerate}
Sections~\ref{sec:related}--\ref{sec:setup} give related work, the exposure
channels and the methods, Sections~\ref{sec:atlas}--\ref{sec:defect} the
results, and Sections~\ref{sec:recommendations}--\ref{sec:limitations}
recommendations and limitations.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Leakage in machine learning for science.} Leakage is a recurring
cause of irreproducible results in machine-learning-based science
\citep{kaufman2012,kapoor2023}. Biology has its own guidance on validation
and data splitting \citep{walsh2021dome,whalen2022} and tools for splitting
biomolecular data by similarity \citep{joeres2025datasail}. In structural
biology, standard train--test splits leak through sequence and structural
similarity in protein--ligand affinity prediction
\citep{li2017jcim,volkov2022,li2024lppdbbind,graber2025}, and date-based or
metadata-based splits leave similar overlap in docking
\citep{buttenschoen2024} and protein--protein interaction benchmarks
\citep{bushuiev2024,kovtun2024pinder}. \citet{skrinjar2025} report that
protein--ligand co-folding methods ``largely memorize ligand poses from their
training data''.

\paragraph{Exposure in AlphaFold evaluation.} The AlphaFold2 paper evaluated
the model on PDB chains released after its training cutoff and examined
template similarity \citep{jumper2021}. BETA provides version-specific
benchmark sets with sequence and structure filters \citep{dobson2025beta}.
\citet{terwilliger2024} compared AlphaFold predictions made without PDB
templates with new crystal structures. AlphaFold's predictions of
fold-switched conformations have been traced to memorised training
structures \citep{chakravarty2024}, and retraining with parts of fold space
withheld shows that generalisation survives large omissions but varies with
what is withheld \citep{ahdritz2024}.

\paragraph{Residue correspondence.} The Worldwide PDB (wwPDB) remediated the
archive to address inconsistencies introduced over the years
\citep{henrick2008}. Mapping PDB chains to UniProt has long been non-trivial
\citep{martin2005}. SIFTS provides residue-level mappings between the two
\citep{velankar2013,dana2019}, records them in label numbering and documents
the complications added by missing residues, insertions, expression tags and
linkers \citep{choudhary2023sifts}. Standard sequence alignments often
misalign the regions next to unobserved residues \citep{velankar2013}.
PDBrenum renumbers PDB files to match UniProt \citep{faezov2021pdbrenu}.
Where these resources address how to map residues correctly, we document
what one mapping mistake does to a downstream analysis.

\paragraph{Conformal prediction for structure.} Split conformal prediction
gives finite-sample marginal coverage under exchangeability
\citep{vovk2005,lei2018,angelopoulos2023}. Residues of one protein are not
exchangeable with residues of other proteins, and such hierarchical data
need their own guarantees \citep{dunn2023,lee2023}, as do non-exchangeable
data more generally \citep{barber2023}. CalPro applies evidential and
conformal methods to pLDDT \citep{shihab2026calpro}. We do not re-evaluate
CalPro here.

\section{Exposure channels}
\label{sec:channels}

Let $R$ be an experimental reference structure, first released on date $r$,
compared with an AFDB model $M$ of the same protein. We distinguish three
channels through which $R$, or structures like it, could have informed $M$.
All 138 model files in the first four batches of the preregistered census
are AlphaFold2 models (``AlphaFold Monomer v2.0''). AFDB also serves
ColabFold models \citep{mirdita2022}. Of the 93 model files downloaded for
the post-2022 census (Section~\ref{sec:setup}), 6 are ColabFold Monomer
v1.5.2 models and 87 are AlphaFold Monomer v2.0, and none of the six belongs
to a protein in the exposure comparison of Section~\ref{sec:effect}.

\paragraph{Training.} AlphaFold2 was trained on PDB structures with a
maximum release date of 2018-04-30 \citep{jumper2021}. If $r$ is on or
before that date, $R$ is \emph{training-eligible}: it may have been a
training example. Training also filtered chains and sampled them by cluster,
so eligibility is an upper bound on use of the PDB itself. Self-distillation
on predicted structures is a further route that release dates do not capture
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Templates.} At inference time, AlphaFold2 searches the PDB for
templates. The AlphaFold human-proteome study reports the snapshots it used
of the PDB and of PDB70, a clustered subset of the PDB used for template
search \citep{tunyasuvunakool2021}, and AFDB states that templates were
restricted to structures released before 2021-02-15 \citep{afdbfaq}. AFDB
model files record the PDB entries supplied as templates in the ModelCIF
category \texttt{\_ma\_\allowbreak template\_\allowbreak ref\_\allowbreak db\_\allowbreak details}
\citep{afdbfaq,vallat2023modelcif}, so this channel can be measured for each
model. The list records which templates the network was given, not how much
the prediction relied on them.

\paragraph{Homology.} Even when $R$ post-dates both cutoffs, the same
protein or a close relative may have been in the training-era PDB. This
channel is measured by sequence similarity to the training-era PDB
\citep{jumper2021,buttenschoen2024}, which release dates cannot capture.

AFDB entries also carry a \texttt{modelCreatedDate}, documented as the date
the entry was created \citep{afdbmetadata}. In version~6, unchanged models
received new metadata and the v6 label while their coordinates were carried
over from version~4 \citep{pdbereleasenotes}. The creation date therefore
records neither when a prediction ran nor what it could see, and we use it
only to exclude models created on or after the census start date.

\section{Data and methods}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Terms.} We use \emph{protein} for a UniProt accession, and call
two structures \emph{same-protein} if they map to the same accession. An
\emph{entry} is a PDB entry, which may contain several chains. A \emph{chain
segment} is the set of residues of one PDB chain mapped to one accession.
SIFTS describes it as one or more \emph{SIFTS segments}, each a contiguous
stretch with its own start and end. A residue is a \emph{confident error} if
its pLDDT is at least 90 and its lDDT below 0.60. At the chain level we use a
separate \emph{low-agreement screen}, which flags a chain segment of at least
30 residues with median pLDDT $\ge$ 90 and median lDDT $<$ 0.4. A
\emph{registration} is an analysis plan, with its code and predictions,
frozen in the project repository and timestamped with OpenTimestamps
\citep{opentimestamps}, which anchors a file's SHA-256 hash in the Bitcoin
blockchain. We call an analysis \emph{preregistered} if it was registered
before any lDDT value it uses existed.

\paragraph{ATLAS.} When we queried the ATLAS web resource \citep{atlasweb}
on 2026-09-04, it listed 1{,}938 entry--chain records covering 1{,}735 PDB
entries and 943 proteins, more than the datasets described in the ATLAS
paper \citep{vandermeersche2024}. Of these proteins, 869 have both an AFDB
model and a SIFTS mapping \citep{velankar2013,dana2019}. One has an AFDB
record we could not parse, which leaves 868 proteins and 935
accession--entry pairs. For each reference we took the initial release date,
the first record in the mmCIF category
\texttt{\_pdbx\_audit\_revision\_history} \citep{mmcifdict}.

\paragraph{Preregistered census.} Before any lDDT value existed, we
registered a census design, its analysis code and its predictions. The
design fixes an AFDB version~6 snapshot at $T_0$ = 2026-09-11 00:00 UTC and
collects, in weekly batches, X-ray entries released after $T_0$ at
2.5\,\AA{} resolution or better. Models created on or after $T_0$ are
excluded. Apart from the release-date filter, which places every reference
after AFDB's 2021-02-15 template cutoff, the design adds no exposure
control. What it adds is that every choice in the original design, including
two dated amendments made before any data arrived, was fixed before any lDDT
value was computed. The residue mapping was replaced later, after the error
described in Section~\ref{sec:defect}. We report the first four of 12
planned batches, ending on 4 October 2026: an empty first batch, shortened
to 48 hours by one of the amendments, followed by three weekly ones. With
the original mapping the four batches gave 488 chain segments from 272
accession--entry pairs and 138 proteins, and with the corrected mapping 495
chain segments and 141 proteins (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).

\paragraph{Post-2022 census.} An earlier, exploratory census of 168 X-ray
entries released after 2022-06-02 used the same author-number fallback as
the preregistered census but discarded pairs whose amino acids differed. We
use it to check whether findings recur. It was affected by the same error.
With the corrected mapping, 139 of its entries from 85 proteins are labelled
(96 entries from 64 proteins before), its homology search covered 86
proteins, and 93 AFDB model files were downloaded for it. All its results
outside Section~\ref{sec:post2022} use the corrected lDDT values.

\paragraph{Residue pairing and lDDT.} For each reference chain we pair
C$\alpha$ atoms with AFDB model residues through the PDBe SIFTS UniProt
mapping. We describe the corrected mapping here, and
Section~\ref{sec:defect} the original and its error. The corrected code
(i) indexes the mapping by label numbering (\texttt{label\_seq\_id}) and the
chain's label identifier (\texttt{struct\_asym\_id}), (ii) maps equal-length
SIFTS segments by offset and others by global alignment of the chain's full
deposited sequence to the UniProt sequence, (iii) drops any pair whose amino acids
differ and (iv) rejects a SIFTS segment if more than 10\% of its pairs
differ. Chains with fewer than 30 aligned residues are excluded, as are atoms with an alternative location other than A and modified residues other than selenomethionine (16 and 57 residues of the corrected census). We score each residue by
lDDT-C$\alpha$ \citep{mariani2013}, computed per chain against the AFDB model
over aligned residues with a 15\,\AA{} inclusion radius and
distance-difference thresholds of 0.5, 1, 2 and 4\,\AA. We call the
computation of these per-residue lDDT values \emph{labelling}, and computing
them again from stored inputs \emph{relabelling}. pLDDT is read from the
model's B-factor column and is never used in labelling.

\paragraph{Homology exposure.} For each census protein we queried the search
service of the RCSB PDB (Research Collaboratory for Structural
Bioinformatics; \citealp{burley2023}) for training-era entries in three
classes: (i)~same-protein entries, (ii)~entries containing a chain of a
different accession at $\ge$95\% sequence identity and (iii)~entries
containing one at $\ge$30\% identity. Sequence searches used MMseqs2
\citep{steinegger2017} with an E-value of at most 0.1 and the canonical
UniProt sequence as query. We set no coverage threshold, so a hit, even a
same-protein hit, may cover only part of the protein. Each protein is
assigned its strongest class. A failed query would leave a protein
unresolved rather than novel. Across the
224 proteins queried for the two censuses, 594 lookups returned results (HTTP 200, two of them repeats of an identical query), 408 ran and found nothing (HTTP 204) and none failed
\citep{rcsbsearchapi}. Every request and response was cached and hashed.

\paragraph{Template exposure.} From each AFDB model file we read the
template PDB IDs, mapped each template to UniProt through SIFTS and recorded
its release date. A model has a \emph{same-protein template} if any template
maps to its accession.

\paragraph{Preregistered coverage prediction.} The census registration
includes a conformal-coverage prediction that does not use pLDDT.
Section~\ref{sec:p5} describes it together with its result.

\section{Most ATLAS references predate the training cutoff}
\label{sec:atlas}

For ATLAS we measured only the training channel. We count a protein as
training-eligible when all of its references were released on or before
2018-04-30. Of 868 ATLAS proteins, 791 (91.1\%) are training-eligible in
this sense, and so are 851 of the 935 accession--entry pairs (91.0\%). The
references were released between 1988 and 2023 (median 2008). Only 77
proteins have any reference released after the cutoff, so agreement between
AFDB models and ATLAS references cannot by itself be read as evidence of
generalisation. Using the AFDB creation date as the cutoff instead, a
tempting but wrong proxy (Section~\ref{sec:channels}), would place every
reference before the model for 863 of 868 proteins (99.4\%).

ATLAS was built to describe dynamics, and reusing it to study AlphaFold
error requires an explicit exposure control. The obvious control is to keep
only references released after the cutoff. Section~\ref{sec:homology} asks
how much that achieves.

\subsection{Homology exposure}

Every structure in the census was released more than eight years after the
training cutoff, yet 87 of the 138 proteins (63\%) had a same-protein or
near-identical ($\ge$95\% identity) structure in the training-era PDB, 128
(93\%) had a relative at $\ge$30\% identity, and only 10 (7\%) had none
(Table~\ref{tab:homology}). For same-protein hits, the earlier structure may
cover only part of the protein. Some well-studied proteins have hundreds of
such structures: hen egg-white lysozyme (P00698) had 730 same-protein
entries in the training-era PDB, human carbonic anhydrase~2 (P00918) had 700
and $\beta_2$-microglobulin (P61769) had 693. These hits show only that a
related structure existed, not whether it differs by ligand, mutation,
construct or crystal form.

\subsection{Template exposure}

The template lists give a more direct measure. All 138 model files list four
PDB templates each, released between 1988 and January 2021, so none
post-dates the documented 2021-02-15 limit. For 45 proteins (33\%) at least
one template is a structure of the same protein, for 88 all templates are of
other proteins, and 5 are unresolved (Table~\ref{tab:homology}). The
homology and template channels overlap only in part. Of the 78 proteins with
a same-protein structure in the training-era PDB, 43 were given a
same-protein template and 35 were not. Conversely, two proteins with no
same-protein structure in the training-era PDB were given a same-protein
template (released in 2019 and 2020). One of them, Q9F0J8, has no
training-era relative at $\ge$30\% identity.

\subsection{Is exposure associated with agreement?}
\label{sec:effect}

To ask whether exposure goes with better agreement, we compared proteins of
the post-2022 census with and without a close relative in the training-era
PDB (same protein or $\ge$95\% identity), using lDDT values recomputed with
the corrected mapping (Section~\ref{sec:post2022}). The comparison was
registered in advance as analysis~6 of a separate verification plan in the
repository. For each protein we took the median lDDT and the median pLDDT
over its residues, and for each group we then averaged these per-protein
medians. The 40 proteins with a close relative had a mean median lDDT of
0.975, against 0.959 for the 45 without (difference 0.016, 95\% bootstrap
interval 0.003--0.032). Their mean median pLDDT was also higher, 96.1
against 92.3 (difference 3.8, interval 1.7--6.0), a larger gap than in lDDT
(3.8 against 1.6 points on a 0--100 scale). The mean per-protein share of
confident errors was similar in the two groups (0.12\% and 0.13\%,
difference $-0.01$ percentage points, interval $-0.18$ to $0.17$), although
the interval is too wide to rule out a small difference. We did not run this
comparison on the preregistered census, whose registered predictions about
pLDDT-conditional coverage have not yet been evaluated.

\section{A residue-numbering error created spurious confident errors}
\label{sec:defect}

The exposure measurements need no residue pairing, but any comparison of
agreement does. It needs per-residue lDDT values, and computing them for the
census is where our own pipeline failed. After the fourth batch the
preregistered coverage prediction, which does not use pLDDT, failed at both
levels (Section~\ref{sec:p5}). A post hoc check found that one protein,
K7PQ54, held 14\% of the labelled residues and half of the test residues
that fell outside the 0.90 intervals. Tracing it led to a residue-numbering
error that, unlike exposure, makes the model look worse than it is. The
registration and the stored source files let us confirm the cause and
relabel the census.

\subsection{Mechanism}
\label{sec:mechanism}

In the PDBe SIFTS mapping files we used, each SIFTS segment has a start and
an end in label numbering and, when the boundary residue was observed, in
author numbering. Our census code built each SIFTS segment's residue map
from its starting author number and fell back to the starting label number
when the author number was missing, which happens whenever the first residue
of the segment is unobserved. Our code then looked atoms up by author
number. When the two schemes differed by an offset, every residue of the
segment was paired with the wrong UniProt position, a \emph{register shift}.
The code had no amino-acid identity check to catch it.

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} shows chain~A of entry 9qj6, a structure of
K7PQ54, dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase from the lesser grain borer
\emph{Rhyzopertha dominica} (507 residues; \citealp{uniprot2025}). The SIFTS
segment starts at label residue~1 (UniProt~35), which is unobserved. Author
numbering runs 34 ahead of label numbering, so the atom with author
number~36 (label~2, UniProt~36) was assigned UniProt~70. Under this shift
chain~A had a median lDDT of 0.198 at a median pLDDT of 98.4, and chain~B,
shifted in the same way, had 0.199. With the mapping fixed, chains~A and~B
aligned over 472 and 471 residues with no sequence mismatch and had median
lDDT 0.993 and 0.995.

Q92802, 8~chains), is missing from both sets because the original run never
fetched its reference file. Table~\ref{tab:scope} compares the two sets of

\end{table}

Correcting the mapping changed the median lDDT of 188 of the 488 original
chain segments. For 151 of them, from 40 proteins, the change exceeded 0.2,
and all 151 moved upward (Figure~\ref{fig:scope}). The other 37 changed by
at most 0.056. After correction the low-agreement screen flags no chain
segment, and the 13 entries whose chains had differed from each other by
0.4 or more in median lDDT no longer do.

The corrected set is not a superset of the original. It labels 6{,}465
residue positions that the original did not and drops 916 that it did, a
net gain of 5{,}549, and it adds three proteins and seven chain segments
that the original mapping had rejected. A reimplementation with a separately
written parser and lDDT code reproduced the corrected lDDT values of 36
chain segments (26 targeted, 10 random) exactly. A second check, with a
different mmCIF parser and lDDT implementation \citep{kunzmann2018biotite}
and residue-level SIFTS mappings from PDBe, reproduced 494 of the 495
corrected chain segments, every lDDT value within $5\times10^{-5}$. In the
remaining one, chain~A of entry 21xg, an alignment tie next to two
crosslinked residues makes our corrected mapping pair one residue with the
UniProt position next to the one that residue-level SIFTS gives. This moves
18 lDDT values by up to 0.054 and the chain's median by 0.003, and leaves
the share of residues below 0.60 unchanged.

\subsection{The error mimics overconfidence, and segment checks catch it}
\label{sec:detect}

The error created residues with high pLDDT and very low lDDT, the pattern an
overconfident model would produce. A calibration study run on these values
could have concluded that AlphaFold2 is overconfident on new structures, and
our preregistered coverage test did fail on them.

The precondition, a SIFTS segment that begins on an unobserved residue, is
common: 304 of the 492 SIFTS segments mapped to the 138 original proteins
(62\%) begin this way. It causes a shift only when author and label
numbering are offset at the start of the segment. That was the case for 147
of the 492 SIFTS segments (30\%), from 39 proteins, with offsets from $-31$
to $+201$. In a comparison we added after the fact, all 147 lay within the
151 chain segments whose median lDDT changed by more than 0.2. Each of the
other four of those 151 contains a SIFTS segment whose offset changes
partway along it.

Checking amino-acid identity for every pair is necessary but not
sufficient. Under a register shift only about 5--7\% of pairs match by
chance in the segments we tested, and those pairs pass a per-pair filter.
What catches the shift is the per-pair check together with rejecting any
SIFTS segment in which more than 10\% of pairs differ. The post-2022 census
had only the per-pair check. It therefore lost most shifted residues but
kept those that matched by chance (Section~\ref{sec:post2022}). Coarser
checks catch some cases. A set in which 31.9\% of residues fall below 0.60
lDDT is far from the 2.6\% seen in the corrected post-2022 census, and 13
entries had chains that differed by 0.4 or more in median lDDT. A
chain-agreement check would still miss entries such as 9qj6, where both
chains were shifted alike.

\subsection{The same error in the post-2022 census}
\label{sec:post2022}

In the post-2022 census, 69 entries had been rejected for having too few
aligned residues and 3 because the model could not be downloaded. With the
corrected mapping, 45 of those 69 entries yield lDDT values. In all, 139
entries are labelled instead of 96 (37{,}531 labelled residues instead of
24{,}457). The total is 96 + 45 $-$ 2 because two previously labelled
entries, 10pa and 10ps, are now rejected by the identity checks. The
Spearman rank correlation between pLDDT and lDDT rose from 0.617 to 0.643.
The pooled residue-level share of confident errors, taken over all residues
with pLDDT $\ge$ 90, halved from 0.58\% ($n = 20{,}777$) to 0.29\%
($n = 31{,}032$). In the lowest-confidence bin (pLDDT $<$ 50) the share
below 0.60 lDDT rose from 58\% ($n = 177$) to 75\% ($n = 314$).

The added residues and the rise in the lowest-confidence bin come from the
45 newly labelled entries. Most of the rise in rank correlation, and more
than half of the fall in confident errors, come from recomputing entries
that were already labelled, where shifted residues had matched by chance and
passed the per-pair filter. Entry 10ke, for example, held 22 of the 121 original confident errors. Because the September source files had not been kept,
this recomputation used files fetched again in October 2026.

\subsection{The preregistered coverage prediction before and after correction}
\label{sec:p5}

The census registration includes a prediction that tests the pipeline rather
than AlphaFold2's confidence. Let $y$ be a residue's lDDT and $1-\alpha$ the
nominal coverage. For each of 200 random protein-level splits (seeds
0--199), 50\% of proteins form a fitting set, 25\% a calibration set and
25\% a test set. The fitting set serves only to compute $\hat y$, a single
number: the median lDDT over its residues. Every test residue receives the
interval $\hat y \pm q$, where $q$ is the
$\lceil (n_{\mathrm{cal}}+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$-th smallest of the calibration
scores $|y-\hat y|$ and $n_{\mathrm{cal}}$ is the number of calibration
residues \citep{vovk2005,lei2018}. At $1-\alpha = 0.90$ and $0.95$, we
predicted that mean residue-level test coverage over the 200 splits would be
at least the nominal level minus three Monte Carlo standard errors (MCSE;
Table~\ref{tab:p5}), a threshold that depends on the data. Calibration
residues are pooled across proteins of different sizes, and for such
hierarchical data marginal residue-level coverage carries no finite-sample
guarantee \citep{dunn2023,lee2023}.

On the original lDDT values the prediction failed at both levels
(Table~\ref{tab:p5}). Those values were corrupted, so we withdraw that
recorded outcome as invalid; a dated correction of record in the repository
documents this (Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}). The original outputs remain
archived unchanged. The original MCSE (0.0080 and 0.0057) was more than twice the corrected one, which lowered the original thresholds; K7PQ54 alone held 14\% of the original residues, so whether it fell in the test set could change a split's coverage considerably. On the
corrected values (123{,}454 residues from 141 proteins) both means are
slightly below nominal but pass their thresholds. At 0.90 the mean exceeds
the threshold by 0.0031, about one MCSE. At 0.95 it exceeds it by 0.0007,
less than half an MCSE (0.0017), so this level passes narrowly and could
reverse under a different but equally defensible correction. Counting residues
on the interval boundary as uncovered leaves the pass intact (0.9453 against
0.9449).

The seeds and the decision rule were fixed by the original registration, and
the corrected mapping and the re-evaluation protocol were registered before
the re-evaluation ran. The re-evaluation is not blind. Before registering it, we
computed the same statistic outside the registered pipeline on an early
version of the corrected mapping (122{,}024 residues rather than 123{,}454)
and obtained 0.898 and 0.947. The re-evaluation used the registered
evaluation code, invoked directly while data collection was paused. Two
reimplementations written from the registration, without importing the
evaluation code, reproduced both means exactly, and one also reproduced the
original failure. Together with the mechanism in
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}, this makes the original failure consistent with
a labelling artefact. Because the interval ignores pLDDT, the corrected
result tests the lDDT values and the pipeline. It says nothing about how
well pLDDT tracks error.

Table~\ref{tab:checks} lists the checks that would have exposed the problems
reported here. We recommend the following.
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item Report exposure by channel, and state which channels the question
      requires to be controlled. Release dates alone measure only training
      eligibility, and database creation dates measure none of the channels
      (Section~\ref{sec:atlas}).
\item For AFDB models, read the template list from the model file
      (Section~\ref{sec:homology}).
\item Stratify results by sequence identity to the training-era PDB
      \citep{buttenschoen2024}, or split data by similarity
      \citep{joeres2025datasail} (Section~\ref{sec:homology}).
\item Pair residues in one numbering scheme, check amino-acid identity for
      every pair, reject any SIFTS segment with more than 10\% mismatches,
      and run the coarser checks of Table~\ref{tab:checks} before any
      downstream analysis (Section~\ref{sec:detect}).
\item Treat a failed calibration test as a possible pipeline failure first.
      Ours is consistent with an error in the lDDT values rather than in the
      model (Section~\ref{sec:p5}).
\item Register analyses before lDDT values exist and keep the raw inputs
      (Sections~\ref{sec:scope} and~\ref{sec:post2022}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Other AlphaFold versions, including AlphaFold3 \citep{abramson2024}, have
their own cutoffs. We did not measure exposure through multiple sequence
alignments or self-distillation, and template lists do not show reliance
(Section~\ref{sec:channels}). Homology was measured with fixed identity
thresholds, a single canonical sequence and no coverage threshold. A
$\ge$30\% hit can be a short domain match, which overstates homology
exposure, and a single canonical sequence can miss isoform-specific hits,
which understates it. lDDT values compare single chains with monomer
models and ignore interfaces and ligands in the crystal, which can lower
lDDT for reasons unrelated to exposure. We score C$\alpha$ lDDT, so our values are
not directly comparable with all-atom lDDT.

The census samples new PDB entries, which over-represent proteins that are
crystallised repeatedly, so its exposure shares describe what a post-cutoff
release filter yields rather than the novelty of proteins in general. It is
reported after four of its 12 batches, and the full census may differ.
Because the correction adds and drops residues, the before-and-after
coverage comparison is not made on identical residues
(Section~\ref{sec:scope}). Finally, the numbering error is documented in one
pipeline, ours. The trigger frequencies we report describe this census, and
we have not examined whether any published analysis is affected.

\section{Conclusion}

Agreement between an AFDB model and an experimental structure is evidence
about AlphaFold only if two things are known: what the model could have
seen, and that the residues being compared are the same residues. In ATLAS,
91\% of proteins had only training-eligible references. In the preregistered
census, whose references all post-date the cutoff, 63\% of proteins had a
same-protein or $\ge$95\%-identical structure in the training-era PDB. In an
exploratory comparison, proteins with such a relative had slightly higher
lDDT and higher pLDDT. In our own pipeline a numbering fallback, with no
segment-level identity check to catch it, made accurate predictions look
like confident errors, and a preregistered prediction failed on the
resulting values and held once they were corrected. Registration, stored
inputs and the checks in Table~\ref{tab:checks} are cheap enough to be
routine for AFDB comparisons.
